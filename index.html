Presentation 1 / Fall 2026 / Marat Alikhan

One cup, two lives.

How do NU students use coffee to mark, cross, or blur the boundary between studying and living, on a campus where those two things happen in the same building?

Interpretivist Nazarbayev University Boundary work

01 / Research question

The question

How do NU students use coffee to mark, cross, or blur the boundary between studying and living?

Mark

Coffee makes the line clearer: "this is study time."

Cross

Coffee helps move over the line: "now I switch to studying."

Blur

Coffee mixes the two: studying and living feel like one thing.

Who

NU students

Where

A campus where studying and living happen in the same building

When

Academic year 2026–27

  • Empirical, not normative: it asks how students do it, not whether they should.
  • Answerable: students can describe and show their own coffee routines.

02 / Epistemological positioning

Interpretivist

Why this tradition fits

  • The question is about meaning: what a cup of coffee means to a student at a given moment.
  • The boundary is not out there to measure. Students create it, so their own accounts are the evidence.

What I am not doing

  • No IV/DV and no hypothesis test: coffee is not treated as a cause of study results.
  • No counting of cups: the goal is to understand the logic, not the amount.

03 / Contribution

What this study adds

Coffee as consumption

Coffee as a crossing device

Existing studies ask how much students drink and why. This one asks what coffee does when students move between study and life.

Adults working from home

Students in shared institutional space

Boundary research mostly studies employees. A residential campus blurs the line in a different way.

US and Spanish samples

A Kazakhstani campus

No study looks at coffee and daily boundaries among students in Kazakhstan.

04 / Literature review

From a common drink to an open question

  1. 1

    Coffee is everywhere

    Over 90% of undergraduates in US and Spanish surveys consume caffeine, and coffee is the largest source in both. Mahoney et al., 2019; Riera-Sampol et al., 2022

  2. 2

    Coffee plays a dual role

    Instrumental: some students see coffee as a tool for good grades and later employment. Franke et al., 2012 Social: 68% of US college students drink it for the taste, 39% for drinking with friends and family. Mahoney et al., 2019

  3. 3

    The pivot

    The literature analyses studying and sociability as separate domains. It does not examine how coffee can be a device to move between them, mark the distinction, or blur it.

  4. 4

    Life domains sit on a continuum

    From high segmentation (contrasting roles, hard to cross) to high integration (overlapping roles, easy to cross but hard to mark). People use boundary work to create, maintain, or blur the lines. Ashforth et al., 2000

  5. 5

    Boundary work has forms

    Behavioral, temporal, physical, or communicative. With physical tactics, objects become markers: one participant wore a "collar" to signal a meeting. Kreiner et al., 2009 Coffee could combine several tactics at once, since they complement rather than replace each other.

  6. 6

    The gap

    Research focuses on work and home. Students, whose living and studying are highly integrated in one place, are overlooked, and coffee has not been studied as a device for marking, crossing, or blurring boundaries.

  7. ?

    Therefore: how do NU students use coffee to mark, cross, or blur the boundary between studying and living?

05 / Analytical framework

How I will look

Sensitising concepts

Boundary work

Nippert-Eng, 1996

People actively draw and redraw the line between domains. Makes me notice what students do to separate, not just where they are.

Micro role transitions

Ashforth et al., 2000

Small, repeated acts that help people exit one role and enter another. Makes me notice the moment of crossing: the first cup, the last cup.

Boundary tactics

Kreiner et al., 2009

Makes me notice the different ways coffee might draw a line:

Where: café vs. dorm When: first cup How: the ritual "Let's grab coffee"

Expectations (what I may find, not hypotheses to test)

  • Different boundary tactics may be tied to coffee, even several at once, because they complement each other.
  • Students may use coffee to mark the boundary, to cross it, or to blur it by mixing studying and living, or in more than one of these ways.
  • Coffee may be the ritual for entering study mode but not for leaving it; the exit may be something else.
  • Students may drink coffee just for pleasure, with no boundary involved.

Positionality: an NU student studying NU students

What it gives me

  • I know which places matter at NU.
  • I can see whether living and studying really overlap at NU, or whether students keep them clearly apart.
  • As a coffee enjoyer, I can notice if NU students see coffee's importance differently.
  • I study at NU but live in the city, so I don't live the overlap myself. As a partial insider, I can notice routines that on-campus students take for granted.

What it may miss

  • I don't live on campus, so I haven't felt what it's like to study and live in the same building. I may misunderstand students' experience.
  • As a coffee enjoyer, I may overrate coffee and overlook students for whom it means little.
  • As a fellow student, participants may tell me what they think I want to hear.
  • NU may be less integrated than I expect.

How I worked with AI

The research question, the interpretivist stance, all six sources, the literature review, the expectations and the positionality are my own work. Claude helped me structure the argument of the literature review, drafted the contribution section, wrote the code for this page and condensed my notes into its short text. I reviewed every section and can explain each concept and source.

07 / Ungraded

What AI helped me understand, and what I still don't

Helped me understand

  • Marking a boundary is not the same as crossing it.
  • Students may want integration, not segmentation, so coffee might blur the boundary instead of marking it.

Still unclear

  • How to ask neutral questions that leave room for both marking and blending.
  • How to bring the Kazakh cultural context in with solid sources.

Presentation 1 / Fall 2026 / Marat Alikhan

One cup, two lives.

How do NU students use coffee to mark, cross, or blur the boundary between studying and living, on a campus where those two things happen in the same building?

Interpretivist Nazarbayev University Boundary work

01 / Research question

The question

How do NU students use coffee to mark, cross, or blur the boundary between studying and living?

Who

NU students

Where

A campus where studying and living happen in the same building

When

Academic year 2026–27

  • Empirical, not normative: it asks how students do it, not whether they should.
  • Answerable: students can describe and show their own coffee routines.

02 / Epistemological positioning

Interpretivist

Why this tradition fits

  • The question is about meaning: what a cup of coffee means to a student at a given moment.
  • The boundary is not out there to measure. Students create it, so their own accounts are the evidence.

What I am not doing

  • No IV/DV and no hypothesis test: coffee is not treated as a cause of study results.
  • No counting of cups: the goal is to understand the logic, not the amount.

03 / Contribution

What this study adds

Coffee as consumption

Coffee as a crossing device

Existing studies ask how much students drink and why. This one asks what coffee does when students move between study and life.

Adults working from home

Students in shared institutional space

Boundary research mostly studies employees. A residential campus blurs the line in a different way.

US and Spanish samples

A Kazakhstani campus

No study looks at coffee and daily boundaries among students in Kazakhstan.

04 / Literature review

From a common drink to an open question

  1. 1

    Coffee is everywhere

    Over 90% of undergraduates in US and Spanish surveys consume caffeine, and coffee is the largest source in both. Mahoney et al., 2019; Riera-Sampol et al., 2022

  2. 2

    Coffee plays a dual role

    Instrumental: some students see coffee as a tool for good grades and later employment. Franke et al., 2012 Social: 68% of US college students drink it for the taste, 39% for drinking with friends and family. Mahoney et al., 2019

  3. 3

    The pivot

    The literature analyses studying and sociability as separate domains. It does not examine how coffee can be a device to move between them, mark the distinction, or blur it.

  4. 4

    Life domains sit on a continuum

    From high segmentation (contrasting roles, hard to cross) to high integration (overlapping roles, easy to cross but hard to mark). People use boundary work to create, maintain, or blur the lines. Ashforth et al., 2000

  5. 5

    Boundary work has forms

    Behavioral, temporal, physical, or communicative. With physical tactics, objects become markers: one participant wore a "collar" to signal a meeting. Kreiner et al., 2009 Coffee could combine several tactics at once, since they complement rather than replace each other.

  6. 6

    The gap

    Research focuses on work and home. Students, whose living and studying are highly integrated in one place, are overlooked, and coffee has not been studied as a device for marking, crossing, or blurring boundaries.

  7. ?

    Therefore: how do NU students use coffee to mark, cross, or blur the boundary between studying and living?

05 / Analytical framework

How I will look

Sensitising concepts

Boundary work

Nippert-Eng, 1996

People actively draw and redraw the line between domains. Makes me notice what students do to separate, not just where they are.

Micro role transitions

Ashforth et al., 2000

Small, repeated acts that help people exit one role and enter another. Makes me notice the moment of crossing: the first cup, the last cup.

Boundary tactics

Kreiner et al., 2009

Makes me notice the different ways coffee might draw a line:

Where: café vs. dorm When: first cup How: the ritual "Let's grab coffee"

Expectations (what I may find, not hypotheses to test)

  • Different boundary tactics may be tied to coffee, even several at once, because they complement each other.
  • Students may use coffee to mark the boundary, to cross it, or to blur it by mixing studying and living, or in more than one of these ways.
  • Coffee may be the ritual for entering study mode but not for leaving it; the exit may be something else.
  • Students may drink coffee just for pleasure, with no boundary involved.

Positionality: an NU student studying NU students

What it gives me

  • I know which places matter at NU.
  • I can see whether living and studying really overlap at NU, or whether students keep them clearly apart.
  • As a coffee enjoyer, I can notice if NU students see coffee's importance differently.
  • I study at NU but live in the city, so I don't live the overlap myself. As a partial insider, I can notice routines that on-campus students take for granted.

What it may miss

  • I don't live on campus, so I haven't felt what it's like to study and live in the same building. I may misunderstand students' experience.
  • As a coffee enjoyer, I may overrate coffee and overlook students for whom it means little.
  • As a fellow student, participants may tell me what they think I want to hear.
  • NU may be less integrated than I expect.

07 / Ungraded

What AI helped me understand, and what I still don't

Helped me understand

  • Marking a boundary is not the same as crossing it.
  • Students may want integration, not segmentation, so coffee might blur the boundary instead of marking it.

Still unclear

  • How to ask neutral questions that leave room for both marking and blending.
  • How to bring the Kazakh cultural context in with solid sources.
